# Output formats and audience calibration

Four deliverable shapes, and how to pitch each one at the reader in front of you.

## Who is reading, and what they will do with it

The reader's decision determines what belongs in the commentary far more than the subject matter
does. The same month's results produce five different documents.

| Reader | What they are deciding | Register | Length | What they need that others do not |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Board | Whether management has control of the business, and whether to approve what is being asked | Formal, measured, plainly written — no internal shorthand | Short; detail moves to an appendix | Trend and trajectory, the adverse items stated without softening, and an explicit ask where there is one |
| Lender or covenant reviewer | Whether the obligations will continue to be met | Factual, conservative, no promotional language | Short and specific | Covenant-relevant lines, liquidity separated from cash, and the basis of every forward statement |
| Executive team | What to do next, and where | Direct, operational | Medium — detail is useful here | Attribution to an owner or function, and the size of the decision in front of them |
| Investor or external stakeholder | Whether the trajectory supports their thesis | Professional, balanced, carefully hedged | Short | Composition and quality of results, not just their size; caveats on anything forward-looking |
| Internal operating or department reader | Where their own line landed and why | Plain, practical | Short, focused on their scope | Only their own scope, at a grouping they recognize |

Two rules across all of them. Do not adjust the substance to suit the reader — only the altitude,
the emphasis and the vocabulary; the adverse items appear in every version. And where a single
document serves several of these readers, write to the most demanding one and accept that some
readers will skim.

## Format 1 — Executive summary

Used when the reader will read one thing and act on it. Sits at the front of a pack, or stands alone
as an email or a briefing note.

Shape, in order:

1. **The headline sentence.** The answer, with the number and the direction, in one sentence. If a
   reader stops after this sentence they should still know how the period went.
2. **Two to four supporting sentences.** The largest drivers, each with its figure and its cause.
   One sentence per driver. This is where most of the value is and where most drafts overrun.
3. **One forward sentence.** What this means for the next period or the full year, hedged to the
   evidence behind it.
4. **One ask, if there is one.** A decision needed, an approval, a risk requiring attention — stated
   as a specific request with a size and a date, not as a general concern.

Length: roughly 100 to 200 words. A summary running past a page has become a report and has stopped
doing the job the reader wanted.

What stays out: line-by-line coverage, methodology, anything the supporting table already shows,
and any qualification that does not change the reader's action.

## Format 2 — Board MD&A prose

Used for the narrative section of a board or investor pack, where the reader expects continuous
professional prose rather than fragments.

Cover in this order, dropping any section the input cannot support rather than padding it:

- **Opening assessment** — the period in aggregate, with the two or three facts that define it, and
  the comparison basis stated.
- **Revenue** — the result, the movement against the spine baseline, and the drivers separated into
  volume, rate and mix. Where composition matters to the reader, the mix and its direction.
- **Margin and operating costs** — gross margin and its direction with the reason; operating cost
  movement separated into deliberate investment, inflationary pressure and efficiency; the
  significant individual movements.
- **Earnings** — the bottom-line result and how the items above produce it. Where a bridge is
  supplied, the prose walks it rather than repeating it.
- **Financial position and liquidity** — cash, working capital movement, borrowings, and the
  adequacy statement, where balance sheet data was provided.
- **Cash generation** — operating, investing and financing separated, with the relationship between
  earnings and operating cash flow explained where they diverge.
- **Outlook** — near-term expectations, the assumptions they rest on, and what would change them.
- **Principal risks** — the ones that could plausibly change the picture, quantified where the input
  supports it, and paired with what is being done about each.

Paragraph discipline: one idea per paragraph, three to five sentences, opening sentence carrying the
point. Every paragraph contains at least one figure. No paragraph exists solely to transition
between two others.

What belongs in an appendix rather than the prose: the full comparison table, the metric definitions,
the detailed bridge, and the scope and basis notes.

## Format 3 — Bulleted flux commentary

Used beside a variance table, usually at month-end, usually consumed by a controller, an auditor or
a reviewer working line by line. The reader is not reading for pleasure; they are checking that each
material movement has an explanation they can accept.

One bullet per material line, each with the same internal shape:

- **Line item** — as labelled in the accompanying table, not a synonym.
- **Movement** — amount and percentage, against the named baseline.
- **Driver** — the specific cause, in business terms, with its own figure where the movement splits
  across more than one cause.
- **Timing or permanent** — and if timing, when it reverses.
- **Action or owner** — only where one exists. Not every line has one and inventing one is worse
  than omitting it.

Keep every bullet to one or two sentences. Order by size of movement, or match the table's own
order if the reader will be reading them side by side — ask which, rather than choosing and forcing
the reader to hunt. Where the table's own order is used, the largest adverse movement is still
called out in the opening line, so following the table never has the side effect of burying it.
Close with a single line covering the lines that fell below the materiality rule.

This format is the easiest of the three to write badly, because the structure will happily accept
"driven by timing" as a driver. It is not one. The driver names what shifted and why it shifted.

## Format 4 — Account note

Used under a drill-down of one account or account class — who or what drove it, by business
account, subaccount segment or branch, month by month — when the tables already exist and the reader
wants the story in a paragraph. Typically the write-up the account drill-down skill's output needs,
or the per-account note an executive asks for after a flux.

Shape, in order, 80 to 150 words:

1. **The figure and the movement.** The account (or class) in the window against the comparison,
   in the account's natural sign, with the tie-out stated ("ties to the GL").
2. **The drivers, named.** The two to four business accounts, segment values or branches that
   explain most of the movement, each with its amount and its pattern — new, lost, step, spike,
   credits, irregular billing — dated where the date is the finding.
3. **What the ledger cannot attribute.** The share with no business account, not visible, or in an
   unassigned segment, stated as a share, never spread.
4. **The open question and who answers it.** A pattern is evidence; the reason behind it is a
   hypothesis until a contract, an invoice or the line's owner confirms it. Say which, and name the
   next step.

What stays out: the tables themselves, codes the reader has to decode, and any cause the tables do
not show.

## Choosing between them

- The reader will act on one thing → executive summary.
- The document is a governance or external artefact and expects continuous prose → board MD&A.
- The commentary sits next to a table and will be reviewed line by line → bulleted flux.
- The subject is one account's drivers, and the drill-down tables are already on the page → account
  note.
- More than one is genuinely needed → write the flux commentary first, because it forces the
  per-line reasoning, then compress it upward. Compressing down from a summary is where invented
  drivers get in.

## Numbers, tables and rendering

Numbers in prose: millions as `$X.XM`, thousands as `$X.XK`, ratios as `X.X%`; full numbers with
thousands separators in table cells, where precision matters more than brevity. Never show a raw
internal record ID — it is meaningless to the reader and the ERP's own UI never shows it either. Table cells carry full precision with thousands
separators. Percentages state their base where it is not obvious. Once a rounding convention is
chosen it holds across the whole package, including the appendix.

Tables support prose; they do not replace it, and prose does not read a table aloud. Where a table
and the prose disagree, the table is usually right and the prose has drifted — check both before
delivery.

Markdown is the default output. A rendered HTML deliverable — a dashboard, a styled report — follows
the `velixo-design` skill bundled alongside this one, which carries Velixo's palette and logo rules and
applies to rendered artefacts only, never to plain commentary text.
